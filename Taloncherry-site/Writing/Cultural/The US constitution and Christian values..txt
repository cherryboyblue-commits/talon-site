---
title: The United States Constitution, and Christianity.
date: September 2nd 2023
---
The United States Constitution, drafted in 1787, stands as a cornerstone of American identity. While widely recognized as a secular document, the Christian worldview of many Founding Fathers deeply influenced its principles. From human dignity to checks and balances, Christian thought helped shape the constitutional framework. Yet the Founders balanced this with Enlightenment ideals to ensure religious freedom without state control. Today, however, misinterpretations of the First Amendment have led to the removal of religious texts and teachings from schools and government, often going beyond original intent. This exploration examines how Christian ideology molded the Constitution and where modern rulings have veered off course.

Christian Roots in the Constitution
At the heart of Christian belief is the idea that humans are created in God’s image, endowed with inherent dignity and rights. This resonated with the Founders, many of whom were Protestant Christians, and it is reflected in the Preamble's emphasis on securing the blessings of liberty. This aligns with Protestant teachings on personal responsibility before God and dovetails with Enlightenment thinkers like John Locke, who argued that governments exist by the consent of the governed—a concept mirroring biblical covenants.

The Christian view of human nature also played a key role. Figures like James Madison, influenced by Calvinist ideas of human imperfection, understood that people are capable of both virtue and vice. This shaped the system of checks and balances, dividing power among the legislative, executive, and judicial branches to prevent tyranny. Madison’s argument in The Federalist Papers that ambition must counter ambition reflects a Christian skepticism of human nature, ensuring no single authority could dominate.

Christian morality also underpins the focus on justice and the common good. While the document avoids religious language, its call to promote the general welfare echoes Christian ethics of fairness and community. John Adams famously said the Constitution was made for a moral and religious people, suggesting that values like honesty and civic duty, rooted in Christianity, were vital for a functioning republic.

A Secular Framework with Purpose
Despite these influences, the Constitution is secular because it avoids mentioning God or any specific religion and includes safeguards like the First Amendment Establishment Clause: Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof. Article VI also bans religious tests for office, ensuring governance is not tied to faith. These choices reflect the Founders’ experiences with religious conflicts in Europe and colonial America, where state-backed churches often led to persecution. By crafting a secular document, they created a government based on reason and universal principles, making it inclusive for a diverse society.

Misinterpretations of Separation
The First Amendment was meant to protect religious freedom by preventing the government from establishing an official religion or forcing anyone to follow one. The Founders, who frequently included prayers in government sessions and referenced God in the Declaration of Independence, did not aim to erase religion from public life. However, modern interpretations have stretched this principle, turning it into a tool to suppress religious expression.

This shift began with the Supreme Court decision in Everson v. Board of Education (1947), which applied the Establishment Clause to state and local governments and leaned on Thomas Jefferson’s wall of separation phrase from a private letter. Later rulings took this further:

Engel v. Vitale (1962) banned voluntary school prayers, even non-denominational ones, claiming they endorsed religion.

Abington School District v. Schempp (1963) outlawed Bible readings in schools, ignoring the view that religion supported moral education.

Stone v. Graham (1980) and McCreary County v. ACLU (2005) removed Ten Commandments displays from schools and courthouses, treating them as unconstitutional endorsements.

These decisions often relied on tests like the Lemon test, which asks if a government action has a secular purpose or avoids promoting religion. Critics argue this creates a double standard where secular ideologies are permitted in public spaces while religious symbols are scrubbed, fostering hostility toward faith rather than neutrality. This goes beyond the original intent to prevent coercion, not ban religious influence outright.

Finding Balance
The Constitution’s secular nature was a deliberate choice to protect freedom, not to erase the Christian values that shaped its core ideas of liberty and justice. Recent Supreme Court shifts, such as rulings supporting religious funding for charter schools, suggest a move toward the Founders' original vision of a government neutral toward religion rather than antagonistic to it.

By understanding the Christian roots of the Constitution and the missteps in modern interpretations, we can better appreciate the balance the Founders struck. They created a system where faith could inform public life without dominating it, ensuring a republic that serves all citizens. Honoring that legacy means fostering a public square where religious voices can contribute without fear of exclusion.
